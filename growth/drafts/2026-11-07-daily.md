**Where the numbers come from:** `npv` and `irr` in `src/lib/finance/tvm.ts` (which count the first flow as time 0, the correct convention). Example project: −₹1,000 today, then ₹300, ₹400, ₹500 and ₹200 over the next four years, discounted at 10%.

| Formula | What it does | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `=B1 + NPV(10%, C1:F1)` | keeps the year-0 outlay outside NPV() | **₹115.57** (correct) |
| `=NPV(10%, B1:F1)` | discounts every flow, including the outlay, one extra year | ₹105.06 |

The wrong answer is the right one divided by 1.10, so it is always off by exactly one period of discounting (here ₹10.51, about 9%). On a project with a negative NPV the same mistake makes it look less bad: −₹1,000 then 250, 300, 350, 200 gives −₹125.23 correctly but −₹113.85 with the trap. The IRR (15.32% for the first project) is unaffected, because `=IRR()` treats its first value as time 0. The `project-npv` problem isn't on any contest list, and no contest problem is used.

> Excel's NPV function has a trap that catches students and analysts alike.
>
> A project costs ₹1,000 today and returns ₹300, ₹400, ₹500 and ₹200 over four years. Discount rate 10%.
>
> Most people type: `=NPV(10%, B1:F1)` → **₹105.06**
> The right answer: `=B1 + NPV(10%, C1:F1)` → **₹115.57**
>
> Why the gap? NPV() assumes the first value in the range arrives one period from now. Put today's ₹1,000 outlay inside it and Excel discounts that too, so every number is pushed a year into the future. The result is always the true NPV divided by (1 + rate).
>
> Three habits that avoid it:
> 1. Keep the time-0 cash flow outside NPV().
> 2. Use XNPV with real dates when flows aren't evenly spaced.
> 3. Sanity-check one cash flow by hand: 300 / 1.1 = 272.73.
>
