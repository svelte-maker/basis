**Title:** Why "average return" overstates what you earn: two funds, same 8% average, ₹12k apart on ₹1 lakh

**Body:**
> I keep seeing returns compared as simple averages, so here's a worked example of why that misleads, with numbers you can check in a spreadsheet.
>
> **Two hypothetical funds, ₹1,00,000 each, five years**
>
> | Year | Fund A | Fund B |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | +25% | +9% |
> | 2 | −20% | +7% |
> | 3 | +30% | +10% |
> | 4 | −10% | +6% |
> | 5 | +15% | +8% |
>
> Both have an arithmetic average of exactly 8.00%.
>
> - Fund A ends at ₹1,34,550. CAGR = (134550 / 100000)^(1/5) − 1 = **6.11%**.
> - Fund B ends at ₹1,46,870. CAGR = **7.99%**.
>
> **Why:** returns multiply. A −20% year after a +25% year takes you back to exactly where you started (1.25 × 0.8 = 1), even though the two years "average" +2.5%. The more a fund swings, the further its CAGR falls below its average. A rough rule is CAGR ≈ average − (standard deviation)² / 2.
>
> **Drawdowns:** Fund A fell 20% from its peak in year 2. Recovering from a loss L needs L / (1 − L): 20% needs 25%, 50% needs 100%.
>
> **What to do with this:** when comparing funds, use CAGR over the same dates (factsheets already show multi-year returns as CAGR), and look at the worst drawdown alongside it. A fund sold on its "average return" deserves a second look.
>
> Not investment advice; the funds are made up to keep the maths clean. Happy to answer questions.
