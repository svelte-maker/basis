**Where the numbers come from:** `multiples()` in `src/lib/finance/comps.ts` and `enterpriseValue()` in `src/lib/finance/corporate.ts`. Illustrative companies, not real ones. Both have EBITDA ₹200 crore, D&A ₹50 crore (EBIT ₹150 crore), tax 25%, no cash, and an enterprise value of ₹1,500 crore.

| | Company A (no debt) | Company B (₹750 cr debt at 8%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest | ₹0 | ₹60 cr |
| Net income | ₹112.5 cr | ₹67.5 cr |
| Equity value (market cap) | ₹1,500 cr | ₹750 cr |
| Enterprise value | ₹1,500 cr | ₹1,500 cr |
| **P/E** | **13.3x** | **11.1x** |
| **EV/EBITDA** | **7.5x** | **7.5x** |

Why B's P/E falls: its earnings yield is 7.5% (A: 112.5 / 1,500), and debt costs 8% × (1 − 25%) = 6% after tax. Replacing equity that "costs" 7.5% with debt that costs 6% lifts earnings per rupee of equity to 9% (67.5 / 750), so P/E falls to 1 / 9% = 11.1x. Same shortcut as the 6 Nov accretion/dilution drafts, but a different question and different hooks.

> One company trades at 13.3x earnings. Another at 11.1x. Which is cheaper?
>
> Trick question. They're the same business.
>
> Both earn ₹200 crore of EBITDA. Both have ₹50 crore of D&A and pay 25% tax. Both are worth ₹1,500 crore as a business (enterprise value).
>
> The only difference: Company B borrowed ₹750 crore at 8% and used it to replace half its equity.
>
> Company A: net income ₹112.5 cr, market cap ₹1,500 cr → P/E 13.3x
> Company B: net income ₹67.5 cr, market cap ₹750 cr → P/E 11.1x
>
> B looks 17% cheaper on P/E. Nothing about the business changed. Only the financing did.
>
> Now look at EV/EBITDA: ₹1,500 / ₹200 = 7.5x for both. Identical, as it should be.
>
> That's the interview answer to "Why use EV/EBITDA instead of P/E for comps?":
> → EV and EBITDA both belong to all capital providers (debt and equity), so the multiple ignores how the company is financed.
> → P/E mixes in interest and leverage, so it moves when the capital structure moves.
>
> Bonus point most candidates miss: B's P/E fell because its after-tax cost of debt (8% × 0.75 = 6%) is below A's earnings yield (7.5%). If debt cost more than that, leverage would push P/E *up*.

> Interview Q: "Why EV/EBITDA over P/E?"
>
> 30-second answer:
> • EV and EBITDA are both before financing, so the ratio ignores debt vs equity.
> • P/E is after interest, so leverage moves it.
> • Example: add ₹750 cr of 8% debt to a ₹1,500 cr business → P/E 13.3x → 11.1x, EV/EBITDA stays 7.5x.
